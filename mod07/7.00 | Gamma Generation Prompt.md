# Gamma Generation Prompt
## Unit 7 | Mythology & Proverbs — Final Unit Overview

---

**Use this prompt verbatim in Gamma's AI generation field.**

---

### PROMPT

Generate a presentation for a 10th grade English class called **"Unit 7 | Mythology & Proverbs."** This is the unit overview students will see on Day 1. The tone should be direct, wry, and conversational — like a real teacher talking to students, not a textbook. No filler. No generic AI language. Every slide should feel purposeful.

Use a dark navy (`#1A1A2E`) and white color scheme as the primary palette, with blue (`#2E5D9E`), green (`#2E7D4A`), and red/burgundy (`#8B1A1A`) as accent colors for section headers and callout blocks. Clean typography. Minimal decoration.

---

**Generate exactly these slides in this order:**

---

**SLIDE 1 — Title Slide**

Title: *Unit 7 | Mythology & Proverbs*
Subtitle: *What are stories for?*
Unit duration: 5 weeks
Body text: "Myths are arguments about human nature, told as stories. Proverbs are the same arguments, compressed to a sentence. This unit is about learning to hear the argument inside the story — and then making one yourself."

---

**SLIDE 2 — The Big Question**

Header: *The Question We're Asking*
Display this question large, centered, as the focal point of the slide:
*"What does a story have to do before it becomes wisdom?"*

Below it, smaller: "Everything we do in this unit is preparation for answering that question — with a myth, a proverb, and your own voice."

---

**SLIDE 3 — What a Myth Is Actually Doing**

Header: *Myths Aren't Just Old Stories*
Two-column layout:

Left column — "What people think myths are":
- Ancient entertainment
- Explanations for weather, fire, death
- Stories about gods doing dramatic things

Right column — "What myths actually are":
- Arguments about what humans fear most
- Explanations for why people fail each other
- A culture's attempt to make sense of suffering, pride, and consequence

Bottom callout box (dark navy background, white text):
*"Every myth is a culture making a claim. Your job this unit is to figure out what the claim is."*

---

**SLIDE 4 — Proverbs as Compressed Myths**

Header: *The Proverb Is the Myth That Survived*
Body: "A proverb is what's left after a myth gets distilled down to its essential argument. Same idea. Fewer words. More pressure."

Show this pairing as a visual example:

| MYTH | PROVERB |
|------|---------|
| Icarus flew too close to the sun. His father warned him. He didn't listen. He fell. | "Pride goes before a fall." |

Below the table:
"Both are making the same argument. The myth gives it a body. The proverb gives it teeth. Together, they're evidence."

---

**SLIDE 5 — The Three Levels (L1 / L2 / L3)**

Header: *How Deep Are You Reading?*
Subtitle: *Every question — and every answer — lands somewhere on this scale.*

Three-column layout, each column a distinct accent color:

**Column 1 — L1 (blue `#2E5D9E`)**
Label: *Surface*
"What happened? Who did what? When?"
Example: "What punishment did Prometheus receive?"
Note: "Start here. Don't stay here."

**Column 2 — L2 (green `#2E7D4A`)**
Label: *Psychology*
"Why did they do it? What were they afraid of? What did they want?"
Example: "Why did Prometheus steal fire knowing what would happen?"
Note: "Most students live here. It's good work. Keep going."

**Column 3 — L3 (burgundy `#8B1A1A`)**
Label: *Claim*
"What does this say about human beings — not just this character?"
Example: "What does Prometheus's choice argue about the relationship between knowledge and consequence?"
Note: "This is where myths come alive. This is where your Myth Talk has to land."

---

**SLIDE 6 — Sub Days Overview (May 8, 9, 11)**

Header: *While I'm Out — You're Not Off*
Subtitle: *Three days. Three episodes. Three written responses.*

Body: "You'll be watching Joseph Campbell's *The Power of Myth* (PBS, 1988) — three episodes over three days. Campbell is one of the great myth explainers of the 20th century. Each day has a viewing sheet. Each viewing sheet has a written response due. Bring all three to class on May 12."

Three-column episode list:

**May 8 — Episode 1**
*The Hero's Adventure*
"What does every hero story have in common — and why does that pattern keep showing up across cultures that never met each other?"

**May 9 — Episode 2**
*The Message of the Myth*
"What job is a myth doing that a science textbook can't do?"

**May 11 — Episode 3**
*The First Storytellers*
"Why did humans start telling stories in the first place — and why haven't we stopped?"

---

**SLIDE 7 — The Myth Talk (Culminating Task)**

Header: *The Myth Talk — Your Summative*
Subtitle: *3–4 minutes. One myth. One proverb. One argument.*

Body: "You're going to stand up and make an argument about what a myth and a proverb — together — say about human beings. Not a summary. Not a book report. An argument."

Four-step visual (numbered, sequential):

1. **Pick a myth** — any culture, any tradition. Real mythology only.
2. **Pick a proverb** — any culture or language. Surprising pairings welcome.
3. **Find the claim** — one sentence that says what they share.
4. **Give the talk** — open with the myth, name the claim, unpack the proverb, make the L3 move, land it.

---

**SLIDE 8 — The Rubric**

Header: *How You're Graded*
Subtitle: *25 points total. Five categories.*

Display as a clean table:

| Category | Points | What It Means |
|----------|--------|---------------|
| Claim Clarity | 5 | One specific, arguable sentence about what the myth and proverb share. |
| Evidence from the Myth | 5 | You use the story — specific moments, characters, details. Not just plot summary. |
| Evidence from the Proverb | 5 | You unpack it. Explain what it means and why it belongs here. Quoting alone doesn't count. |
| The L3 Move | 5 | You land on a truth about human beings, not just ancient stories. |
| Presence | 5 | Talking to the room, not reading at it. You sound like you believe it. |

Bottom note: *"The hardest category is The L3 Move. It's also the one that makes the talk worth listening to."*

---

**SLIDE 9 — Checkpoint Dates**

Header: *The Timeline*
Display as a visual timeline or clean date list:

- **Today (May 5–6)** — Unit launch. You learn where we're going and what the Myth Talk looks like.
- **May 8–11** — Sub days. Watch the film. Fill out your viewing sheets. Write your responses.
- **May 12** — Back together. Discussion of Campbell. Bring all three viewing sheets.
- **Week of May 19** — Myth + proverb chosen. Claim sentence written and shown to Finn.
- **Week of May 26** — Myth Talks. Sign up for a day. Notes okay, script not okay.

---

**SLIDE 10 — What Makes a Myth Talk Work**

Header: *Weak vs. Strong — What It Actually Sounds Like*
Subtitle: *Same myth. Same proverb. Different results.*

Two-column comparison (use contrasting colors — red for weak, green for strong):

**WEAK (red-tinted card)**
"I'm going to talk about Icarus and the proverb 'Don't fly too high.' Icarus flew too close to the sun and died. The proverb means the same thing. Both are about being careful."
*What's missing: No claim. Plot summary instead of argument. The proverb is restated, not unpacked.*

**STRONG (green-tinted card)**
"Icarus and 'Pride goes before a fall' are both making the same argument: human beings are wired to want more than they're allowed, and cultures need a story to explain why that impulse gets punished."
*What works: One arguable claim. Both texts used as evidence. Gets to a truth about people.*

---

**SLIDE 11 — Closing / Call to Action**

Header: *Start Here*
Body: "Before anything else — find a myth that actually interests you. Not the first one you think of. One that feels unresolved. One that makes you want to argue about it."

Three prompts displayed as large cards:
- *"What story keeps showing up in your head that you've never been able to explain?"*
- *"What proverb did someone tell you that you've never totally believed?"*
- *"What human flaw do you think every culture is secretly obsessed with?"*

Bottom: "Your Myth Talk starts with one of those questions. Come to class tomorrow ready to talk."

---

**Design notes for Gamma:**
- Keep each slide focused on one idea. Don't crowd.
- Use the dark navy header bars for section labels the way a document would use a banner.
- The L1/L2/L3 slide and the Rubric slide should feel like reference cards — scannable, not decorative.
- The Weak vs. Strong slide should feel like a side-by-side diagnostic, not a judgment.
- Avoid clip art, stock imagery of ancient ruins, or generic "mythology" aesthetics. Clean and functional beats decorative.
