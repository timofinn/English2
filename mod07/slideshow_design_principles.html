Presentation Skills

Make a Deck That Doesn't Suck

A quick guide to visual design principles for student presentations — before you open a blank slide and start typing.

Most bad presentations aren't the result of bad ideas. They're the result of not thinking about design at all — opening a blank slide, picking a random color, and typing everything you want to say directly onto it.

This page gives you some design principles behind what makes a slide deck actually work. Read it before you build. It'll take five minutes and save you an hour of "why does this look terrible?"

First: Pick the Right Tool

⭐ Recommended

Canva

Start from a template. All the hard work of design (color, layout, fonts) has already been done for you. You only need replace the content. Pretty neat.

canva.com → search "presentation" → pick one that fits your topic's vibe.

The Old Standby

Google Slides

Fine — but use a theme from the "Theme" menu, not a blank deck. A blank deck with no theme is where bad slides come from.

File → Theme → browse. Pick one. Commit to it.

Advanced

Gamma / Beautiful.ai

AI-assisted decks that auto-layout your content. Great if you're comfortable with the content and just want it to look polished fast.

gamma.app — paste your outline, pick a style.

The Principles

One Idea Per Slide If you can't say it in a headline, it's two slides.

The most common mistake: treating a slide like a page in a Word document. A slide is not a document. It's a visual anchor your audience can reference as you present it aloud.

[CLIP ART HERE]
ADAPTABILITY, SURVIVAL AND PRACTICALITY!!!
Just as a dancer must listen to the changing beat of the drum and adjust their steps, be flexible. When we insist on clinging to outdated methods of survival in the face of change, we fail. This is very true and important. Life is full of unexpected turns. It ebbs, it flows. Resist change and be left in the dust. This part is VERY important to understanding the cultural value of the proverb!!

Three ideas. A wall of text. A clip art box. A title with three exclamation marks.

The music always changes.
The question is never whether it will.
The question is whether you'll move with it.

One idea. One image. One line the speaker can land on. The rest is said out loud.

Visual Hierarchy The eye needs a path. Give it one.

Visual hierarchy means the most important thing on the slide is obviously the most important thing. Your eye should land on the title first, then the supporting content, then the caption or detail.

This is broken when everything is the same size, the same color, and the same font. If everything is emphasized, nothing is.

Title: 36–44pt, bold Body: 20–24pt max Caption/detail: 14–16pt, muted color Never: same size for everything Never: center-align body text
Color Palette Three colors max. Everything else is noise.

Pick a palette before you start. Most Canva templates already have one locked in — that's the whole point of using a template. If you're building from scratch, limit yourself to three colors: a background, a primary text/element color, and one accent for highlights.

The other rule: contrast. Light text on dark background. Dark text on light background. Never gray on white, never yellow on white, never red on blue.

Dark + Teal + Fog ✓ Clean contrast, professional feel
White + Midnight + Gold ✓ High contrast, elegant accent
Yellow + Red + Blue + Green + Pink ✗ Five colors. This is a crime.
Typography Two fonts max. Choose with intention.

A slide deck should have two fonts: one for headings, one for body text. That's it. They should feel related — one can be more decorative, the other should be highly readable, even in smaller sizes.

Never: more than 2 fonts Never: decorative fonts for body text Never: ALL CAPS walls of text
Visuals One strong image beats ten bullet points.

Every slide needs a visual element. Not a floating clip art PNG from 2003 — a real, intentional image that supports the idea on the slide.

Full-bleed images: cover the whole side or half the slide Use Unsplash or Pexels (free, high quality) Search specifically — "Nigerian market crowd" not just "Africa" Never: stretch or distort an image Never: blurry or pixelated images Never: floating clip art with a white background box

In Canva: search images directly inside the editor. In Google Slides: Insert → Image → Search the web. Both pull from licensed, high-quality sources.

White Space Breathing room isn't wasted space. It's design.

Cramming content edge to edge is the visual equivalent of talking without pausing. The empty space around your text and images is what lets the content breathe — and what makes the important stuff feel important.

A slide that looks "too empty" to you will look perfectly balanced to your audience. If you think a slide looks sparse, you're probably right on target.

Minimum 0.5" margin on all sides Don't fill every inch of space Fewer elements = more impact per element
Consistency A deck that looks like a deck, not a ransom note.

Every slide should feel like it belongs to the same presentation. Same fonts. Same colors. Same spacing. Same general layout structure. This is why Canva templates work — they enforce consistency automatically.

When you change fonts, colors, or layouts slide-by-slide, the audience spends mental energy adjusting to the new visual — instead of listening to you.

Pick a template and stick to it Same title size every slide Same layout pattern for content slides Never: different background color each slide "for variety" Never: slide transitions set to a different effect each time
Speaker Notes The slide is for them. The notes are for you.

Speaker notes live below the slide and only you can see them during a presentation. They're where your full sentences go — the slide gets the headline, the notes get the rest.

If you find yourself putting long paragraphs on the slide, stop. Move that content to the speaker notes. Leave the slide with one line or one image. Say the rest.

"The music always changes."
Speaker Notes (only you see this) Ask the class what they think the music represents before moving on. Wait for a few guesses. Then explain: the Igbo used this proverb to talk about survival — not dance. The music is whatever life throws at you. Your job is to move with it, not freeze. Transition: "So let's look at a story that shows exactly what happens when someone refuses to change their dance..."

In Google Slides: click "View → Show Speaker Notes." In Canva: click the notes icon below each slide. In Gamma: each card has a notes field.

Slide Count & Slide Count Discipline Fewer slides, more depth. Always.

More slides does not mean a better presentation. A 20-slide deck where every slide is a bullet wall is worse than 6 slides with one idea each, delivered confidently.

The presentation is you. The slides are support. If you removed all the slides and just talked, the presentation should still make sense. If it doesn't — you're reading from slides, not presenting.

Aim for roughly 1 slide per minute of talk time Title slide + 4–6 content slides is often enough Never: a separate slide just for "Thank you!" Never: reading directly from the slide word for word

Before You Submit

The Squint Test + Checklist

Squint at each slide from arm's length. If you can't tell what the main point is — redesign it. Then run through this list.

Every slide has one clear main idea
I used two fonts maximum, consistently across all slides
My color palette is 3 colors or fewer
Every slide has at least one image or visual element (not clip art)
Text is not covering the entire slide — there's breathing room
All slides look like they belong to the same presentation
I can read every word from the back of a room (minimum 20pt)
I added speaker notes so I don't have to memorize or read from the slide
I can present this without looking at the screen the whole time

The slides are a backdrop.

You're the presentation.
